The investment appeal of the Phuket property market is DEBATABLE¹

Phuket is heavily marketed as a high-yield market. Before buying to earn an income, once the risk of an unfinished project has been ruled out (the developer and its finances checked, independent due diligence done), a few things are worth considering.

  1. Advertised yield and real yield are different figures. Advertising usually quotes a gross yield of 8–15% from short-term rental. After the management company fee (usually 20–30%), common area fees, repairs and tax, the net yield, by the agencies' own estimates, is around 4–7% a year. Electricity is a separate significant cost: the management company resells it to you at its own rates.
  2. Guaranteed yield is prepaid rent. Developers promise 5–10% a year for several years, but this money is usually already built into the price. When the guarantee ends, the market sets the income.
  3. A buy-back promise is worth as much as the company that made it. The developer promises to buy the apartment back after a few years at an agreed price. This is a contractual obligation, not a state or bank guarantee. Check who exactly gives it: the developer itself, a separate project company or a related firm with no assets. Find out what secures it: a bank guarantee, collateral or nothing. If the company goes bankrupt or is liquidated before the buy-back date, the buy-back claim becomes an ordinary debt in the general queue of creditors.
  4. Part of the price is the intermediary's fee. Phuket's primary market is almost entirely agent-driven: by the author's observation, about 9 out of 10 new builds are sold through agents. The developer pays them on average about 10% of the property's value and builds this cost into its budgets. For comparison, on the resale market an agent's commission is usually 2–3%. Bear in mind that some agents are motivated to sell the specific property they have an agreement with the developer on, not the one that suits you best. For the same reason agents more often offer off-plan properties rather than finished resale ones, even if a resale property would suit you better: the commission on a new build is several times higher. So it makes no sense to decide on a purchase based only on an agent's recommendations: the agent has an interest in the sale. The exception is if you find an agent you pay yourself (a buyer's agent): then they will represent your interests. Some developers offer discounts if you come to them without an agent.
  5. Daily rentals in condominiums are restricted by law. The Hotel Act B.E. 2547 prohibits renting out accommodation for less than 30 days without a hotel licence. Most condominiums do not have such a licence, and since 2025–2026 the Phuket authorities have been checking violators more often. Renting for a month or longer is legal, but the income from such rentals is noticeably lower.
  6. There is a lot of supply. According to a review by REIC, the Real Estate Information Center of the state Government Housing Bank, presented in September 2026, more new units came onto the Phuket market in the first half of 2026 than were sold. The unsold stock exceeded 17,000 units, more than 13,000 of them condominium apartments. About 2,000 apartments were sold in the half-year. At the current pace of sales, it could take up to 6 years to sell the houses (not condominiums) already on offer. REIC itself concludes that the market has not "stalled" but is becoming more selective: buyers choose more carefully and selling takes longer.
  7. Selling is harder than buying. Buyers on the resale market are mostly the same foreigners, limited by the 49% quota. Leasehold loses value every year, and if you sell within 5 years (of registering the right) the seller pays a 3.3% specific business tax.
  8. Dependence on tourism and the exchange rate. Demand relies on tourists and the season (the rainy season runs roughly from May to October), and every tourist season is different. A drop in arrivals from the main countries or a change in the baht exchange rate immediately affects income.
  9. Properties wear out quickly. A tropical climate with high humidity, downpours and salty sea air ages property faster than a temperate climate. Within a couple of years a room may develop a damp smell and mould, especially if waterproofing and ventilation were skimped on. Metal and appliances corrode, finishes and facades fade and crack. Budget for regular repairs: both the rent and the resale price depend on wear.

This does not mean you cannot make money in Phuket. You can, but only selectively: properties in the best locations with a hotel licence and professional management can generate a stable income. Another opportunity is buying at the pre-sale stage, when the developer sells the first apartments at a launch price before active sales begin. With a proven developer and a good location, such an apartment can be sold for more even before the building is completed. But the risk of an unfinished project is highest here, and reassignment of the contract is often restricted by the developer's terms or requires an extra fee.

RULE: calculate net yield and exit costs before you buy. Decide to buy when the potential benefits outweigh the risks.

RULE: compare not the price of the property but the price per square metre and the expected rent per metre. A high price per metre ≠ a high income.

RULE FOR CHOOSING A PROPERTY: the higher the competition in a segment, the higher the requirements for the property's competitive advantages. A property should not merely meet market standards but have measurable advantages over its competitors in the key parameters: price per square metre, location, developer reliability, liquidity, construction quality, legal clarity and running costs.

MY VIEW: a purchase in Phuket is best treated as buying a place to live with possible income, not as a reliable investment. If you plan to live in the apartment yourself and save on rent, consider alternatives: buying on the resale market or an apartment in a new build by reassignment.

— Lina Lee

A simplified calculation. The resident's tax is calculated only on rental income, without other income or personal deductions. Land and building tax is calculated on the purchase price, not the cadastral valuation. Price growth, exchange rates and exit costs are not included; you can calculate exit costs on the "Selling and exit" page.

⁴ Payback (payback period) is how many years it takes for the income from a property to return the amount invested: purchase price ÷ income per year. Effective payback is calculated from net income, that is after the management company fee, common area fee, utilities, repairs, insurance and taxes. Advertised payback is based on the yield promised by the developer or agent: 100 ÷ advertised yield. Growth in the property's price, inflation and exit costs are not included here.

⁵ Effective here means estimated: payback calculated on the net income that actually remains with the owner after all costs and taxes. Advertised means calculated on the yield promised by the developer or agent. This is not a standard economic term but a label used on this site; in economics this indicator is called the "payback period".

⁶ How the calculator works. 1) Rental revenue: for daily rentals, average nightly rate × 365 days × occupancy; for monthly rentals, rent per month × number of months. 2) The management company fee is deducted from revenue. 3) The owner's costs are deducted: common area fee (rate per m² × area × 12 months), electricity and utilities, repairs (a percentage of the purchase price), insurance and land and building tax (0.02% of the purchase price). 4) Tax on rental income is deducted: for a non-resident, 15% of the amount paid out by the management company; for a resident, the progressive scale applied to 70% of that amount (30% is the standard expense deduction). 5) What remains is net income for the year. Net yield = net income ÷ purchase price. Effective payback = purchase price ÷ net income per year; advertised payback = 100 ÷ advertised yield.
