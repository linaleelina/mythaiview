Circular flow of money

Goods, services and labour move one way, money the other. Cash passes from hand to hand, while non-cash money always goes through banks.

How to read the diagram

  • Green arrows are goods, services and labour. Businesses sell goods and services to households, and households work for businesses.
  • Blue arrows are non-cash money. A payment by card, PromptPay QR code, transfer or e-wallet is debited from the buyer's account and credited to the seller's. Between banks such payments go through the ITMX system, large settlements through BAHTNET at the Bank of Thailand. Wages, taxes and benefits usually also move as non-cash payments.
  • Orange dotted arrows are cash. Banknotes are issued only by the Bank of Thailand, which supplies them to commercial banks, and they in turn to people through tellers and ATMs. Cash is paid from hand to hand, without a bank.
  • Rest of the world. For Thailand it is a major source of money: exports, tourism, foreign investment and remittances from abroad bring foreign currency into the country, which banks exchange into baht. In the other direction go import payments and transfers abroad. International transfers pass through SWIFT and correspondent banks (in detail on the "Bangkok Bank" page).

THE MAIN ECONOMIC PRINCIPLE: money is a means of exchange that keeps goods, services and labour moving between participants in the economy.

  • An excess of money relative to goods and services can lead to rising prices and inflation.
  • A shortage of money and effective demand can lead to falling sales, output and employment.
  • An uneven distribution of money can disrupt money flows even when the total amount of money in the economy is sufficient.
  • Fast-moving money is not in itself a sign of health. Turnover driven by debt and speculation can be very active and end in a crisis — that is how the 1997 crisis began in Thailand.

A healthy economy is not simply a large amount of money but a sustained flow of money, goods, services and income between all participants, backed by growth in real production and not only by debt.

Savings return to circulation

Money does not disappear when a person puts it on deposit. It moves from active circulation into savings. Banks lend to businesses and households, and the money returns to economic circulation.

An important nuance: when a bank makes a loan, it does not hand out other people's deposits but credits new money to the borrower's account. This is how most non-cash money in the economy is created. The Bank of England explains this, for example, in its article "Money creation in the modern economy" (2014). How much a bank can lend is limited above all by the capital and liquidity requirements set by the Bank of Thailand, its policy rate and the demand for loans.

How savings finance the economy
Household savings → Bank deposits → Business loans → Investment and wages → Household income ↺
This link not only closes the circular flow of money but also shows how the economy is financed. The diagram is simplified: in reality a loan itself creates a new deposit, and deposits matter to a bank above all as a stable source of liquidity.
